Pipeline

Stage timings

Wall clock per stage, in execution order. Validation runs twice on purpose: once to decide what is fit to transform, once to prove the transformation introduced nothing.

Two of eight stages are 86% of the run. That concentration is what makes incremental validation worth having — a cache hit drops validate from 2.76 s to 0.08 s.

Volume

What each stage moved

A stage that consumed 19,631 frames and emitted 12 raises no exception. Recording volume is how that becomes visible.

The gate rejected nothing and dropped 2 episodes at the quality filter — 19,631 → 18,668 frames.

Validation cost

Where the checking time actually goes

Per-check duration accumulated across both QC passes. Before this existed, timing stopped at the eight pipeline stages, so the dominant stage was internally opaque.

Two video-touching checks are 96.8% of all validation time, and they run adjacently over the same episode pass — a strong hint they decode overlapping work and could share a decoder. The observability profiler itself is the 1.2% row.

Coverage

Modality presence

Computed from what episodes declare, not from the manifest — a modality listed in the manifest and absent from every episode is exactly the silent failure worth catching.

Force and tactile channels are absent from this rig, so the tail-ratio panel below covers only joint state and action.

Cross-modal alignment

The pillar the canonical five don’t have

Freshness, volume, schema, distribution and lineage all pass on a dataset whose video is shifted against its state. The file is complete, typed, non-null and statistically unremarkable — and ruined.

Measured on every episode the alignment check evaluates, not only on failures — a metric that appears only when something is broken cannot show you that nothing is. One frame period at 30 fps is 33 ms; the healthy reading sits five orders of magnitude inside it.

Sensor health

Tail ratio, not tail level

max ÷ p99.5 per channel. On the bimanual ALOHA rig a force channel’s maximum is 2.4× its p99.5 — and that is a successful grasp. Alerting on the level would page on every contact event.

The signal is a shift in this ratio, never its value. Same lesson that forced the value_range recalibration, where percentile bounds fired on all 50 episodes because ~1% of data is tautologically outside a 99th percentile.

Video

Signal without decoding petabytes

This dataset is 470 MB stored and 36.2 GB decoded. At 3–10 PB/year a strategy that decodes everything costs more than the training it supports, so health is tiered.

The first run already produced a per-camera signature: rgb.top is sharp and nearly still, rgb.wrist is blurrier and moves three times as much — which is what a wrist camera does. Tier 3 (embeddings, VLM) is documented and deliberately not built.

Release contract

Clean data that stopped being comparable

A controller silently rescaled by 1.35×. Every value stays finite, timestamps stay monotonic, the schema is unchanged.

A check reports what it saw, a gate decides about an episode, a contract decides about a version. Thresholds here were calibrated against the baseline's own null, not borrowed.

Cardinality

Why episode ids are never metric labels

Identical facts, stored two ways, at the planning scale of 344M episodes per year. Log scale — each gridline is 1,000×.

A time-series database pays for cardinality at write time and holds the index resident; a columnar store pays at query time and holds nothing. The rule that follows is enforced in code, not documented: the metric layer raises on episode_id rather than accepting it.

Drift calibration

Why the conventional threshold is not used

PSI’s familiar 0.1 / 0.25 thresholds come from credit-risk practice, where samples run to tens of thousands. Applied to 50 episodes with 10 fixed bins, the estimator’s own sampling variance dominates.

Null distribution, 200 trials
Calibrated detector, measured

At 50 episodes the median null PSI is 0.301 — above the “significant drift” line — so most comparisons of a distribution against itself would alert. An alert built on it is noise that looks like a working detector until somebody checks. Calibration against the baseline’s own null brings false positives to 0.5% while keeping 94–98% detection at one standard deviation.
